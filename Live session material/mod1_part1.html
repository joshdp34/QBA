Instructor Guide — Period 1 (9/4, Minutes 0–50): Introduction to Business Analytics & Framing Business Questions

NoteHow to use this guide

This is a facilitation guide for the instructor, not student-facing reading. It pairs with the slide deck week_01_04_slides.qmd. Students arrive having completed the pre-session materials: ?@sec-week01 (software setup), ?@sec-week01-02 (the analytics decision cycle), and ?@sec-week01-03 (importing data, observational unit, variable types). This period should feel like a continuation of that reading, not a repeat of it.

Session Objectives

By the end of this period, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the difference between descriptive, predictive, and prescriptive analytics.
  2. Explain why every analytics project starts with framing a decision, and turn a vague business concern into an answerable question.
  3. Recall and apply the categorical/quantitative and discrete/continuous distinctions from the pre-reading to a new example.

Detailed Run of Show

Welcome and Framing (5 min)

Open by naming the arc of the whole course explicitly: every module we cover will map onto the same seven-step analytics decision cycle from the pre-reading. Today is about steps 1 and 2 (frame the decision, obtain appropriate data) plus the descriptive vocabulary (data types) we need before we can do anything else.

Ask the room: “Show of hands—who got through all three pre-session readings?” This isn’t a gotcha; it tells you how much you can lean on the pre-reading versus how much needs re-explaining live.

What Is Business Analytics? (10 min)

Introduce the three-part taxonomy used throughout the course:

  • Descriptive analytics — what happened? (tables, graphs, summary statistics — Module 1)
  • Predictive analytics — what is likely to happen? (regression, forecasting — Modules 4 and 5)
  • Prescriptive analytics — what should we do about it? (optimization — Module 6)

Emphasize that inferential statistics (confidence intervals, hypothesis tests — Modules 2 and 3) is the connective tissue that lets us move from descriptive claims about a sample to justified claims about a broader population or process — which is what makes prediction and prescription trustworthy rather than just plausible-looking.

TipIf the room is quiet

Ask for an example of each type from their own workplace before you supply one. EMBA students almost always have a live example on hand (a monthly sales dashboard = descriptive; a churn model = predictive; a staffing or pricing tool = prescriptive). Real examples from the room land better than manufactured ones.

Framing Business Questions (15 min)

This is the most important segment of the period. The core message: a vague business concern is not yet an analytics question. Walk through the difference using a live example.

Vague concern: “Our customer satisfaction seems to be dropping.”

Framed decision: “Should we change our current return policy for the upcoming quarter, given a hypothesis that a stricter policy introduced in March is driving dissatisfaction?”

Walk through why the second version is usable and the first is not:

  • It names a specific decision (change the policy or not).
  • It names a timeframe (upcoming quarter).
  • It names a candidate explanation to investigate (the March policy change), which tells you what data to go get.
  • It implies what would count as evidence for or against acting.

Use the three framing questions from ?@sec-week01-02 explicitly:

  • What decision is on the table?
  • Who is making it, and what are the realistic options?
  • What would change if the analysis came out one way versus another?
TipA useful gut-check to give students

“If your analysis wouldn’t change anyone’s action, it probably wasn’t the right analysis to run.” Have students apply this test to the vague version above — nobody can act on “satisfaction seems to be dropping” as stated.

Data Types and Measurement Recap (10 min)

This is a recap, not new material — students read ?@sec-week01-03 before class. Move quickly. Cold-call using a dataset different from readiness_store_sales.csv so students have to apply the ideas rather than recite the reading. Suggested example: an EMBA cohort roster with columns student_id, industry, years_experience, undergrad_gpa, completed_capstone (yes/no).

For each column, ask the room to call out: categorical or quantitative, and if quantitative, discrete or continuous. Expected answers:

  • student_id — categorical (a label)
  • industry — categorical
  • years_experience — quantitative, continuous (can include fractional years) — accept “discrete” as a reasonable answer too if a student reasons in whole years only, and use the disagreement as a teaching moment about how measurement precision, not just the underlying concept, affects this classification
  • undergrad_gpa — quantitative, continuous
  • completed_capstone — categorical (binary)

Close by connecting this back to why it matters: every summary statistic, every graph, and every model choice later in the course depends on getting this classification right first.

Small-Group Activity: Frame a Business Question (8 min)

Break into groups of 3–4. Each group gets one prompt (rotate through the four below across sections, or assign by table). Groups have 5 minutes to write a framed decision (not just a topic) and identify one data type they’d need to answer it.

  1. A regional bank is worried about branch staffing costs.
  2. A hospital system is worried about patient no-shows.
  3. A software company is worried about free-trial users not converting to paid subscriptions.
  4. A manufacturer is worried about a rise in product returns.

Cold-call 2–3 groups to share their framed decision. Push back gently if a group’s answer is still a vague concern rather than a decision with options attached.

Debrief and Transition to Break (2 min)

Close with the throughline: “Everything after the break assumes you’ve already framed the decision. Sampling and data collection — up next — only make sense once you know what question you’re trying to answer.” Send students to break.
