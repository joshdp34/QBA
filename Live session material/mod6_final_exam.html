Overview

This exam covers the second half of the analytics decision cycle: building predictive models (Module 4), forecasting a value forward in time (Module 5), and prescribing an optimal action under real constraints (Module 6). It’s built the same way the midterm was: real calculations, real interpretation, and a defensible recommendation, not lengthy hand derivations.

Sections 2–3 return to the same Sample Superstore dataset from the midterm (?@sec-mod3-midterm), so you don’t need to source new data for those parts, just re-download it if you no longer have your copy. Section 6 is a self-contained optimization scenario; all the numbers you need are given directly in this document, no data file is required for that section.

What Your Report Must Include

Your rendered Quarto report should contain the following sections, in this order.

1. Framing (short)

In 2–3 sentences, state a business decision that could draw on all three of this exam’s tools together, a predictive model, a forecast, and an optimization, even though you’ll address each with a separate dataset or scenario below. For example, a retailer might use a model to identify which products are reliably profitable, a forecast to plan next quarter’s demand, and an optimization to decide how to allocate limited shelf space or staffing given that demand.

2. Module 4, Part A: Regression Modeling

Using the Superstore dataset, build a multiple regression predicting Profit per line item from Discount and Category (Furniture, Office Supplies, or Technology), following ?@sec-week09-02’s categorical-predictor approach.

  • Report the fitted equation and interpret each coefficient in plain language (including which category is your reference level and why).
  • Report and interpret \(R^2\) (or adjusted \(R^2\)).
  • Run at least one residual diagnostic (a residual-vs-fitted plot, or a formal test like olsrr::ols_test_breusch_pagan()) and state what it suggests about your model’s LINE assumptions.

3. Module 4, Part B: Classification

Using the same dataset, build a logistic regression predicting whether a line item is profitable (Profit > 0) from Discount (and, optionally, Quantity).

  • Report the odds ratio for Discount and interpret it in plain business language (not just “the odds ratio is 0.85”).
  • Build a confusion matrix at a threshold of your choice, and report accuracy plus at least one metric beyond accuracy (sensitivity, specificity, or precision).
  • Report the AUC and interpret what it says about this model’s overall ability to separate profitable from unprofitable line items.

4. Module 5, Part A: Time Series Forecasting

Aggregate the Superstore data into a monthly total sales time series (sum Sales by year-month across the full dataset). Using this series:

  • Identify whether it shows a visible trend, seasonality, or both.
  • Fit at least two forecasting methods (for example, seasonal naive and one of moving average / exponential smoothing / regression-based forecasting), and forecast total sales for the next three months.
  • State which method you’d actually recommend using, and why, referencing a forecast accuracy comparison (?@sec-week12-03) if you have enough history to hold out a test period, or a qualitative justification if you don’t.

5. Module 5, Part B: Scenario Analysis

Using your forecast from Part A as the base case, build base, optimistic, and pessimistic projections for total sales over the next 12 months, following the approach from ?@sec-week12-03’s Example 12.17 and the 12/4 live session’s Ceres analysis. State the specific assumption behind each scenario (for example, a different assumed growth rate, or a different assumed persistence of a recent trend), not just an arbitrary adjustment.

6. Module 6: Linear Optimization

NoteA craft brewery’s weekly production decision

A small craft brewery produces two products each week: IPA and Lager, sold by the keg. Each keg of IPA contributes $80 toward fixed costs; each keg of Lager contributes $60. Production uses three shared resources:

  • Brewing tank time: each keg of IPA requires 3 hours, each keg of Lager requires 2 hours. 300 hours are available this week.
  • Fermentation capacity: each keg of IPA requires 4 hours, each keg of Lager requires 4 hours. 480 hours are available this week.
  • Packaging time: each keg of IPA requires 1 hour, each keg of Lager requires 2 hours. 200 hours are available this week.

The brewery wants to decide how many kegs of each to produce this week to maximize total contribution.

  • Define the decision variables, objective function, and constraints, in both words and math.
  • Solve the model (in Excel Solver or R), and report the optimal number of kegs of each product and the resulting weekly contribution.
  • Identify which resource(s) are binding and which (if any) have slack, and state how much slack.
  • One sensitivity question: using your Sensitivity Report (or lpSolve’s compute.sens = TRUE output), a supplier offers the brewery 40 additional hours of brewing tank time for $700. Should the brewery accept? Show the calculation that justifies your answer.

7. Executive Recommendation

A short paragraph (4–6 sentences), written for a manager, not a statistician, that ties your findings together. Address: what your regression and classification models suggest about which line items tend to be profitable, what your forecast and scenarios suggest about near-term demand, and what your optimization result suggests about which resource the brewery (or, if you prefer, the retailer from Sections 2–5) should prioritize if it wanted to expand capacity. Be explicit about uncertainty where it applies, and avoid statistical jargon.
